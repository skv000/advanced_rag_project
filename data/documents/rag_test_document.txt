Introduction to Hindu Philosophy.

Hindu philosophy contains several major traditions that explore questions about reality, consciousness, knowledge, ethics, and liberation.

Advaita Vedanta is a non-dualistic school of Hindu philosophy associated particularly with Adi Shankara. Advaita Vedanta teaches that ultimate reality is Brahman and that the individual self, Atman, is ultimately not separate from Brahman. The tradition emphasizes knowledge and realization of this non-dual reality as a path toward liberation.

Samkhya is one of the classical philosophical systems of India. Samkhya explains reality through two fundamental principles: Purusha and Prakriti. Purusha represents consciousness, while Prakriti represents material nature. According to Samkhya, understanding the distinction between consciousness and material nature is important for liberation.

Yoga philosophy is closely related to Samkhya but places greater emphasis on disciplined practice. The Yoga Sutras traditionally associated with Patanjali describe practices involving ethical discipline, physical and mental control, concentration, meditation, and ultimately liberation.

Bhakti traditions emphasize devotion and a personal relationship with the divine. Bhakti can involve prayer, worship, remembrance, singing, and surrender. Different traditions may focus their devotion on different manifestations of the divine.

The Bhagavad Gita presents several approaches to spiritual life. These include karma yoga, the path of action; jnana yoga, the path of knowledge; and bhakti yoga, the path of devotion. The text discusses concepts such as dharma, duty, action, knowledge, devotion, and liberation.

Mimamsa is another classical school of Indian philosophy. It is particularly concerned with dharma, ritual action, and the interpretation of the Vedas. Mimamsa philosophers developed detailed approaches to understanding language, knowledge, and religious duties.

Vedanta developed through interpretations of the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita, and the Brahma Sutras. Different Vedanta traditions developed different views about the relationship between Brahman, the individual self, and the world.

Vishishtadvaita, associated with Ramanuja, teaches a qualified non-dualism. It maintains the unity of ultimate reality while distinguishing the individual selves and the material world as real aspects of that ultimate reality.

Dvaita, associated with Madhva, emphasizes a fundamental distinction between God, individual souls, and the material world. It therefore differs significantly from Advaita Vedanta in its understanding of the relationship between the individual and ultimate reality.

Across these traditions, Hindu philosophy contains diverse perspectives rather than a single philosophical position. Different schools provide different approaches to understanding reality, consciousness, ethical action, devotion, knowledge, and liberation.
